I watched this movie very recently, and it is a particularly amazing film. It is the first film to have made me tear up so much. The animation is simple and childish, which conflicts with the harsh, emotional, and brutal depiction of war in the film. The music is also not as unique or powerful as other Studio Ghibli films, but it ultimately makes the moments of silence and uncertainty more powerful. It is a movie with heartbreak and great sadness, leaving no hope on the table. I contemplated my reasons for living after watching this. 

The film is set in the latter half of World War II, when massive air raids and bombing campaigns were being deployed on Japan. However, despite being a war film, the focus is not on the war in a conventional sense. Rarely does the film show the soldiers, tanks, or even bombs being dropped from the skies. There isn’t any conversation about the morality or political complexity of the war. In fact, we as viewers are largely unaware of the political developments of the war at any time in the film, let alone who to be rooting for.

The film instead showcases the effects. The flattening of the landscape and city. The lines of injured and dead bodies. The famine. The selfishness and brutality. The crippling sense of morality and optimism. The film shows how war strips humans of their humanity and what that looks like. 

The film starts with a scene of the central character Seita sitting on the ground, slumped against a pillar, garbed in patched-up pants and a ripped tank top. His head droops over his legs, and his face shows no glimpse of life, telling of the fact that he has been lost for a long time. Around him are similar-looking bodies, lifeless and defeated by the world. The first lines are Seita, in a ghost-like form, acknowledging his death: “September 21, 1945. That was the day I died.” The dramatic irony allows the reader to see the war and story for what it is: that there is no happy ending to this. 

We are first formally introduced to Seita and his little sister, Setsuko, as they are rushing outside of their house. As bombs fall on their house and surrounding areas, they relocate to a shelter and hospital, where they find their mother, who had gone straight to the shelter, injured to the point of incapacitation. 

Seita is a fourteen-year-old kid and is not only confronted with the probable death of his mother. Due to his dad fighting in the war, he is also faced with the burden of protecting Setsuko’s life and innocence. He chooses to lie to her, saying that she would see her mother in a couple of days and that everything was fine. But in such a world that had been so suddenly flipped, nothing was. 

They soon relocate to her aunt’s place, where they find temporary protection and safety. Since Seita doesn’t work, the aunt pesters him about it and his lack of support for her, her husband, and daughter. She pushes him to sell their mother’s expensive clothing for food, and the nagging leads Seita to cook his own food. When she further criticizes his bad habits like not cleaning after himself, Seita decides to leave and decides to live alone with Satsuko.

The aunt is seen as the embodiment of selfishness and is portrayed as apathetic. However, her motives and morals are complex. In a time when food is scarce and fear is constant, the aunt looks after her family first. She makes tough decisions and holds Seita to harsh standards, which is unjust, but doesn’t necessarily come from malice. More than anything, it feels like the aunt seeks to accustom Seita to adulthood, to face criticism with action and make better decisions because of it. 

Yet, Seita’s pride to part from his aunt and lead a life of his own culminates in his downfall. Seita and Setsuko eventually find a small spot far from other people and set up camp. For many days and weeks, they get by, killing frogs, cooking lentils, and selling whatever Seita has to get better food. 

At one point, Setsuko starts to dig a grave for her mother after she learns from the aunt that her mother has passed, leading Seita to cry. It marks the moment that Setsuko starts to mature but also one that shows that Seita couldn’t hide everything. His pride and naive confidence led him to believe the lies he was telling her would never come out, but he finds that his mistakes do have consequences.

Soon enough, rations run out, and a local farmer doesn’t have extra food to sell. With nothing else to barter, Seita, someone who would have become a soldier like his dad, decides to steal. He pulls stalks of sugarcane and beets from the farmer whom he once was friendly toward. During evacuation warnings, he ransacks apartments for clothing. Sometimes, he would even go after his aunt, taking any food and water in the house.

Apart from Satsuko, there are no morally good or bad characters in the film, and Seita, a deeply complex and morally ambiguous character, is a prime example of this. He is a noble, deeply caring guardian who does his absolute best to protect his little sister. Only someone with such focus to save his sister would commit such crimes, but his methods and mistakes he made along the way are horrible and unjustifiable. Only in war would he be stripped of his childhood and forced to make life-and-death decisions. 

The director, Isao Takahata, notes that childhood naivety is a central theme of the story he hoped to convey. He says it’s Seita’s youthful pride and refusal to compromise with relatives that leads to their downfall.

Setsuko’s reality does not change; food becomes scarcer, and she slowly starves until she finally passes away from malnutrition, leaving Seita to grieve in deep despair. 

The movie is based on an autobiographical 1967 book by the same name by writer Akiyuki Nosaka. In reality, Nosaka, depicted as Seita, confessed that he was deeply selfish, felt irritated by his sister's constant crying to the point that he would hit her to stop, and hoarded food for himself when he got the chance. He even said that the first thing he felt when his sister died was a dark sense of relief.

The reality of war is harsher than the characters illustrate, and people in reality would probably do much worse for even less. 

4/5
